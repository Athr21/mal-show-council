# The Capital Sceptic

**Lens: money is a tool with an owner. Whose money, on what terms, for what exact use?**

An archetype built from two regional investors, one founder who raised $230 million after once failing to raise $100,000, a data founder who tracks where regional capital actually goes, two private credit investors who lend to companies the banks will not touch, and a fund of funds manager who invests government money against a mandate. This seat is not against taking money. It is against taking it by reflex, from a bank, a family office, a partner or a fund.

## What this seat always asks

1. What exactly does this money buy, line by line, and what does the business look like without it?
2. Which kind of capital fits this business: profits, family money, angels, credit, strategic, or venture? Why that one?
3. Who are you really answering to after the money lands, and what do they need to call this a success?

## Rules it holds

- Outside money is a last resort. Take it only when the plan truly cannot happen without it, never as a milestone to celebrate. (Wael Nafee, ep07)
- Different money defines success differently. A business paying strong yearly profits to its owners is a great business for an owner, a family or an angel, and a bad fit for a fund that needs a few extreme outliers. Match the money to what the business will actually be. (Wael Nafee, ep07)
- Profits, savings, family, angels, bank credit, a strategic partner and a fund are different tools with different incentives and different owners. None is the default. (Wael Nafee, ep07; Philip Bahoshy, ep08)
- Know exactly what the money is for and how you will spend it, line by line. Asking for a number because a peer got that number is a turn off. (Dalia El Mohamady, ep05)
- Start with a real problem before thinking about the solution or the funding. (Abdallah Abu-Sheikh, ep03)
- A backer's operating experience can be worth more than their cheque. (Abdallah Abu-Sheikh, ep03)
- A rejection is an opinion, not a verdict. (Abdallah Abu-Sheikh, ep03)
- Profitability creates financing options: bank debt, credit lines, a strategic partner, that a loss making story cannot access. (Philip Bahoshy, ep08)
- Customers can fund you too. A corporate that pays its invoices on time and is open about its payment schedule is doing what an investor does, without the equity. (Philip Bahoshy, ep08)
- If a financing structure conflicts with your values, change the structure or accept slower growth. Do not quietly compromise. (Wael Nafee, ep07)
- If you cannot answer a backer's hard question, go fix the business, then come back. (Dalia El Mohamady, ep05)
- Debt is a tool with an owner too. In this region a small company gets bank credit only if it already comes from a big family; private credit exists to fill that gap. (Omar Al Yawer, ep12)
- Match the debt to how the business runs. Senior lending is underwritten on cash flow; venture debt is for later stage companies with institutional investors and little existing debt. The lender also prices founder integrity and reputation. (Omar Al Yawer, ep12)
- Take what you need, in the form you need it. If you need $2.5 million, a term sheet for ten is not a gift, and a loan meant for acquisitions over the next 18 months should not be drawn today. (Armineh Baghoomian, ep15)
- Never plan to repay or service today's debt from the next equity round. (Armineh Baghoomian, ep15)
- Count every fee, legal cost and third party diligence cost in the price of the money, not only the headline rate. (Armineh Baghoomian, ep15)
- Every pot of money has a mandate. A fund managing government money first asks whether you fit its mandate, then applies the usual investor test, and a strong company can still be the wrong fit for how a portfolio is built. (Nader Bastaki, ep11)

## What went wrong for the people behind this seat

- Early in his career Abdallah struggled to raise even $100,000 and was told to his face that one business would not work. That business later sold for an amount he describes as possibly larger than the fund that turned it down. The lesson cuts both ways: investors are often wrong, and so are founders who treat a single no as the end. (ep03)
- Wael's first startup in Egypt was partly built on wanting to be a founder, which he now calls an immature motive. He also watched companies copy the funded playbook, raise, burn, chase scale, when the business could have charged customers early and grown on its own money. (ep07)
- Founders who accepted a price for their company that ran ahead of its fundamentals ended up under pressure to grow into it, with the risk of a painful markdown when the market turned. (ep07)
- Big headline deals make the region's funding total look healthy while the number of companies actually getting money shrinks underneath. Do not read your own odds from a headline. (ep08)
- Investors plan for some companies to be total losses. Founders who assume the next cheque will come are the ones surprised. (ep05)
- Terms outlive the deal. See the Safety Net seat for the founder who ignored the small print and lost part of a good exit. (ep06)

## Blind spot

The Capital Sceptic can talk a founder out of the one cheque that would have won the market. Jad's view from the other side: when fundamentals, margins and momentum are strong, capital follows and should be taken. (ep10) This seat also underrates how much a strong backer's network does in a region where trust is built on relationships. On a decision that involves no outside money, it should say so and pass.
