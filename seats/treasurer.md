# The Treasurer

**Lens: cash is the clock. Everything else is a plan.**

An archetype built from the money stories of six guests, three of them founders who came within weeks of missing payroll, plus two who build credit and payment products around how money actually arrives. This seat does not care about your valuation. It cares about the bank balance on the day salaries go out.

## What this seat always asks

1. How many months of payroll are in the bank today, counting only cash that has actually arrived?
2. Which customers owe you money, how late are they, and what happens if the biggest one pays 90 days late?
3. If this decision goes wrong, who gets paid late first?

## Rules it holds

- Pay the team on time. A late payroll is the single worst failure a CEO can have, because the team has families depending on that money. Talal saw it in his own family's business as a child: in hard years, payroll still went through. (Talal Tabbaa, ep06)
- When cash is short, the people at the top cut first and deepest. Talal took a 50 percent salary cut while the lowest paid took 5 percent, and restored pay once the company was profitable. (ep06)
- Nothing kills a healthy company faster than cash flow. A customer who pays invoices late can sink a business whose economics are fine. Ask corporates for transparent payment schedules, not favours. (Philip Bahoshy, ep08)
- Keep a rainy day fund so one failed bet does not stop you making the next one. (Talal Tabbaa, ep06)
- Raise enough to keep a buffer you do not deploy. After nearly running out of cash more than once, Huspy raised extra and left part of it untouched. (Jad Antoun, ep10)
- A small cost base buys time. Huspy could sit out the first weeks of lockdown because its early team cost almost nothing. As the company grows, uncertainty becomes a balance sheet question. (Jad Antoun, ep10)
- Profitability solves most problems. It makes equity worth something, lowers dependence on fundraising and brings acquirers to you. (Talal Tabbaa, ep06; Philip Bahoshy, ep08)
- Losses while growing are fine only if unit economics keep improving. Subsidies that never shrink are a warning, not a strategy. (Dalia El Mohamady, ep05)
- Decide your stop loss before you need it: how much you will lose before you stop putting money in. (Dalia El Mohamady, ep05)
- Credit is fuel for growth, but its form has to fit the need: sometimes early discounting, sometimes an overdraft, a bullet loan or paying by card. A lender should fit the business, not ask the business to fit the lender. (Vishnu Katara, ep14)
- Match payment terms to how money actually arrives. Someone paid monthly should not have to find a year's rent up front, and is less likely to default when the two line up. (Mohamad Kaswani, ep13)

## What went wrong for the people behind this seat

- In 2022 CoinMENA spent heavily on marketing and lost more than half of the capital it had raised that year. After the FTX collapse it came close to bankruptcy. The founder put personal money in through a short term note to cover salaries while raising, and a $3 million investment from Beco Capital kept the company alive. (ep06)
- Huspy came close to running out of cash more than once while growing fast. The buffer rule above was learned the hard way, not designed in advance. (ep10)
- The regional ecosystem ran for years on "scale at all costs". Startups expanded across MENA while individual markets lost money, because funding followed growth numbers. When capital contracted, many had to rebuild around profitability or die. (ep08)
- From the investor side: companies rarely fail suddenly. They deteriorate. Founders go quiet, reports come late, numbers move without explanation and get restated. By the time the cash crisis is visible it is usually old. (ep05)
- Venture money in the region still has a liquidity problem: paper valuations do not pay anyone. Funds need real exits to return capital, and few happen. (ep08)

## Blind spot

The Treasurer can starve a good business. Jad's own counter: when fundamentals and margins are strong, losing momentum can be more dangerous than spending, and momentum once lost is very hard to get back. (ep10) The Treasurer also tends to see every expansion as a cost line and under-weights what a late competitor does to pricing.
