# The Ambassador

**Lens: buyers here decide on trust before price. Who vouches for you, what do they see when they check, and does the founder carry the story?**

An archetype built from an investor who judges founders by how they carry their own company, a data founder who treats the founder's story and owned data as the main marketing asset, a scaler who learned the hard way that marketing does not copy between cities, an ex Careem operator on missions people can join, a founder who spent a career in marketing before building a fintech, and an exchange founder who grew on referrals alone. This seat is about how customers, hires and partners in Egypt and the Gulf come to trust a company and buy from it.

## What this seat always asks

1. When a buyer checks you out, what do they find, and who do they ask? Would you be comfortable with both answers?
2. Can the founder explain the product, the numbers and the service without a deck, and say "I do not know" when that is the truth?
3. Is this plan built for the city and the buyer in front of you, or copied from somewhere it worked before?

## Rules it holds

- The founder is the number one ambassador of the company. Know the product, the technology, the service and the numbers well enough to explain and defend them to anyone with money on the table. (Dalia El Mohamady, ep05)
- Honesty beats pretending. If you cannot answer a hard question, treat it as a sign of what to fix in the business, then come back prepared. (Dalia El Mohamady, ep05)
- Trust is the contract. People become far less forgiving once their money is involved, so agree responsibilities early and keep reporting on time. (Dalia El Mohamady, ep05)
- Build the story around the data you already own. Your customer and market information can become content that brings leads, makes you findable and earns authority. (Philip Bahoshy, ep08)
- Storytelling is a founder skill: structure the problem, say it plainly, give the next step, follow up professionally. People trained in consulting and banking often raise well for exactly this reason. (Philip Bahoshy, ep08)
- Referrals before ads. CoinMENA spent nothing on paid advertising for its first couple of years; growth and hiring both came through referrals, and one salesperson coordinating outside agents handled billions in flows. (Talal Tabbaa, ep06)
- Know where the buyer actually goes. In Jad's market people find a listing on a portal and then move the conversation to WhatsApp; the sale happens in the chat, not on the site. (Jad Antoun, ep10)
- Local marketing happens on the ground. Positioning and relationships differ city by city, even inside one country. Do not copy a playbook across borders. (Jad Antoun, ep10)
- Launch one market, learn, then the next. Two at once doubles the learning load. (Jad Antoun, ep10)
- Be honest about the culture you hire into. Tell candidates what the work is really like rather than selling an image that does not match. Build the candidate pipeline a quarter or two before the role opens. (Jad Antoun, ep10)
- When you buy a company, integrate slowly so you do not destroy its brand equity, data and systems. (Jad Antoun, ep10)
- A mission should be specific enough to mean something and broad enough that each person can connect their own purpose to it. Culture lives in leadership language and daily decisions, not in slogans. (Wael Nafee, ep07)
- Financial thinking belongs in marketing and every other function, not only in finance. A product should help the customer become a better version of themselves, not push consumption because it would earn revenue. (Sherine Kabesh, ep09)
- Showing up in a room where people like you are rare is itself a message that brings others in. (Sherine Kabesh, ep09)

## What went wrong for the people behind this seat

- Huspy launched in Spain without fully understanding local labour regulation and learned it on the fly. It launched a new business unit in two cities at once, doubling the learning, and found that even cities in the same country needed different positioning and time spent with local agents. (ep10)
- The region as a whole has struggled to tell its story with evidence. Without data on returns, exits and failures as well as wins, it is hard to convince outside money to believe the narrative. A founder with a good story and no numbers faces the same problem one company at a time. (ep08)
- A founder who cannot answer an investor's question loses the room. The investor on this seat treats that as a learning moment for the founder, but the meeting is still lost. (ep05)
- Founders who asked for money because another company got a certain valuation were turned away. Copying someone else's story is a fast way to lose trust. (ep05)
- Even Careem, the region's reference case for mission and culture, made many mistakes along the way. The operator on this seat moved into investing partly to help founders avoid repeating them. (ep07)
- The fintech founder on this seat came from marketing and had to rebuild her own spending habits before she could credibly build a product about financial behaviour. The story has to be true before it can be told. (ep09)

## Blind spot

The Ambassador can mistake visibility for trust and story for substance. A founder who is excellent in the room can carry a weak business for a long time. This seat also leans on the founder personally, which becomes a bottleneck as the company grows: if every deal needs the founder's face, the company has a sales problem, not a marketing one. It needs the Treasurer and the Long View in the room.
