# Ep 4 · Dr. Angel Wesley

Dr. Angel Wesley is an American corporate lawyer, long settled in the UAE, who now runs legal at Mal, a Sharia compliant fintech built on AI from the start. The first half covers her path into Islam, raising Emirati children and why the UAE became home. The second half gets into how Mal tries to return Islamic finance to its principles, what "AI native" means under regulation, and how to serve people the big banks pass over.

## Key takeaways

- [00:04:06] Her first real contact with Islam was a summer of NGO work in Senegal, where a host family fed anyone who walked in off the street as a matter of faith. Seeing values practised daily moved her more than any argument.
- [00:10:14] She did not convert on a single epiphany: the more she learned, the more it matched the moral code she already held, which is why she accepts the word "revert".
- [00:17:56] Expats stop treating the UAE as a two year contract once they marry, buy homes and raise children there; she reads that shift over almost 20 years as proof the country has rooted everyone, not only citizens.
- [00:25:02] Programmes such as the Dubai cultural understanding centre started mixing between Emiratis and expats on purpose, and shared interests (charity, sport) then carried it further on their own. She met her husband doing volunteer work.
- [00:30:00] Her moral code rests on justice, honesty, gratitude and charity, and she argues some things are plainly right or wrong rather than all relative.
- [00:34:43] Before Mal she ran her own legal consulting firm for 12 or 13 years, running legal departments for large clients, then went looking for one in-house role with smart people and real impact.
- [00:36:25] Lawyers long joked that Islamic finance was conventional finance with new labels: products were copied, renamed and shipped because nobody was sure how they would fit a fast growing global market.
- [00:38:11] Mal's stated difference is to start from the principles. Sometimes the answer still comes out as a standard murabaha or ijara, but it is reached by reasoning from the roots with Sharia counsel, not by relabelling.
- [00:40:26] Making money is not the problem in Islam; the test is how. Her four tests: the deal must benefit both sides, be transparent about how the provider earns, not be predatory, and rest on something real (so no synthetics).
- [00:41:42] Fees are allowed because a service is being provided, as long as they are reasonable in total and do not compound like interest. She refuses to call any of this easy, only possible.
- [00:43:39] Mal attracts talent through the founder's track record, a record seed round, a clear ethos and attention to detail in interviews, and the pull of building with AI. Not everyone on the team is Muslim.
- [00:46:11] Building several regulated entities at once (an onshore bank, an FSRA entity, rules abroad) means meeting strict regulator standards, which is why such institutions are rarely built purely on technology.
- [00:47:12] AI watches transactions in real time where banks usually flag them by hand after the fact. A human always reviews, every step is auditable, and nobody can quietly cover up a mistake or a fraud.
- [00:49:00] As chief legal officer she will not let anyone call Mal a bank until it holds a licence. Until then it is a fintech with its own stack, and the team is trained to speak carefully.
- [00:50:26] She says AI has made service worse in many places: she spent two weeks stuck in a chatbot loop before reaching a person. A chat with a human is fine; a bot that solves nothing is not.
- [00:51:57] The app is meant to become personal over time, so two users may see very different interfaces depending on family, needs and the products they use.
- [00:53:32] Underserved people are excluded for three reasons: the economics look thin, parts of the group are rated high risk, and products cannot be structured legally for them.
- [00:55:18] Banks treat migrant workers as a remittance play. Mal asks why they send money home (often land or a house) and wants to help them buy or build directly, because stories of money sent home and lost are common.
- [00:57:05] Small margins can still work at volume across millions of workers abroad. Risk is handled with the usual screening, legal handles cross-border structuring, and AI cuts the cost of running the bank so more risk is affordable.
- [00:58:26] They expand step by step: a product has to work as tech, as a business case and as a legal structure, launch in one market, prove itself, then roll out elsewhere.
- [01:01:09] Speaking for herself, not Mal, she sees demand for ethical banking among young Americans who doubt they will do better than their parents. User experience comes first; the ethics should appeal beyond Muslims.
- [01:04:34] The Sharia board structure stays, but who sits on it decides everything: a board will only do what its members are inclined to do, so they look for scholars excited by digital banking who will brainstorm, not just sign.
- [01:07:19] For anyone exploring a faith, she warns that bad actors exploit seekers online. Check sources, ask trusted bodies such as mosque imams or Awqaf, and travel if you can.
- [01:12:02] Expats in the UAE often cannot get compliant car, home or personal finance unless they work for a large company. Mal wants to drop tests such as "is your employer registered with us" and add tools to track spending against your values.

## Stories and mistakes

- [00:11:47] She had to formally convert at the Abu Dhabi court to get an Umrah visa, which pushed her from private belief to a formal step.
- [00:28:15] In 2008 someone told her Emiratis have no food of their own. It showed her how little expats then knew of local culture, and how much the later festivals changed that.
- [00:37:41] Earlier in her career she helped Islamicise existing conventional products for a US investment bank and a family office, the very relabelling habit Mal now says it avoids.
- [00:50:26] Two weeks in a chatbot loop with an office provider before reaching a person: her reference case for AI that makes service worse.
