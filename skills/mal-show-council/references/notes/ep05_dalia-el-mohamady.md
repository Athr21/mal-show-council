# Ep 5 · Dalia ElMohamady

Dalia ElMohamady is CFO of Sawari Ventures, a VC firm investing across Africa and the Middle East, after a career in corporate, telecom and startup finance. The conversation opens on ethics as disclosure, then works through how VCs judge pitches, what due diligence needs, how a fund handles currency collapse and failing companies, and how founders should read the investor relationship. Her steady theme is that systems and openness, not good intentions alone, keep people honest.

## Key takeaways

- [00:03:23] She defines ethics as balancing your goals against the people you answer to: profit and social duty for corporates, growth and users for startups, risk, return and impact for investors.
- [00:06:56] Governance does not stop bad behaviour; people will always inflate figures. Its job is to help you spot it, so you can sleep knowing you did your best to detect it.
- [00:08:15] Her test for a grey decision: how would I feel if this became public? She leans on disclosure to auditors, regulators and LPs, and treats an inspection as a chance to fix things together.
- [00:16:08] Sleeping well is not proof enough, since some people sleep fine whatever they do. Build systems, colleagues and reporting lines that catch you when you drift; she tells LPs what was done and asks if it is acceptable.
- [00:17:11] When a whistleblower exposed a wrongdoing in a team of about 20 to 25, everyone was investigated, even juniors who protested they were nowhere near it, so the team stood together.
- [00:21:51] She backs confident self-promotion and "fake it till you make it" as ambition, but draws the line at faking it outright. Her example is Builder AI, which she says claimed AI while engineers did the work and revenues proved inflated.
- [00:24:27] Building in public suits founders who are part of the brand; others should not force it. Posting daily without adding value dilutes a brand.
- [00:25:44] Volatile markets are where the biggest returns come from. Due diligence keeps the risk from being reckless, and anyone wanting safety should hold bonds or savings instead.
- [00:27:44] Prepare for due diligence like guests are coming: a tidy data room with legal, financial, contract and policy files, because diligence costs money. Match audit quality to the investor tier, and make it a team effort.
- [00:29:44] A steady business aiming for modest profit, with technology not at its core, is not a venture case. Funds rely on a few large winners to cover the average results and the write-offs.
- [00:31:38] The founder must know the numbers and product inside out, rehearse repeatedly, and treat a tough question as either a chance to convince or a lesson for the next pitch.
- [00:33:04] Her biggest turn-off is an ask with no clear plan for the money. The amount must come from what you will deploy, not from copying another company's valuation.
- [00:34:08] Losses are fine if unit economics improve; endless, rising subsidy is not. If something has value, people should pay for it.
- [00:40:16] In a fund, finance values the portfolio quarterly with fresh eyes, because whoever backed the deal will be bullish. She sees the CFO as co-pilot: warn clearly and early, but the pilot decides.
- [00:42:24] Every investor needs a stop-loss: a set appetite for loss that decides whether to join a follow-on round or accept the loss.
- [00:43:44] Warning signs in a company: founders go quiet, reports arrive late, board meetings repeat the same argument, figures swing without explanation, and restatements pile up. (Transcript labels the first sign Host.)
- [00:46:33] A conventional CFO reports where today's path leads; a strategic one sees the market shifting, as telecoms did when they set up venture arms for payments and fintech. (Transcript labels the start of this answer Host.)
- [00:50:11] Disagree and commit when the leader has real reasons you do not share. Disagree and detach only when you no longer believe the leader acts for the right reasons, and raise it before you leave.
- [00:52:46] Raising in dollars and investing in local currency creates a gap from the first day. The risk cannot be removed, only reduced, through hypergrowth, expansion into other markets, or a unique product with a clear exit. Practical hedges include keeping a dollar cheque unconverted and borrowing in local currency against it.
- [00:56:24] Sharia compliant funding is rarer than funding in general and gets harder as rounds grow. Founders who need it should plan for it from the seed round; she argues states should mandate it on purpose.
- [01:01:41] A professor's model reframed her view: the VC is a gallery, founders are artists, LPs fund the gallery, and the customer is whoever buys the art, meaning the eventual acquirer. Too little time goes on buyers, which she blames for weak distributions. (Transcript labels part of this answer, from 01:02:26, Host.)
- [01:03:44] Founders should build a good company and leave the exit to the investor. A stage investor selling is not rejection; it is graduation.
- [01:05:48] Founders should check the investor too: how often they will call, whether they open doors, help with licences, or stay hands-off. Be professional, since an investor who sees no return will not invest again.
- [01:09:27] Rejection is not about fairness; fairness means there are open channels to be heard. Favouritism exists because people are human, and she accepts connections only when the person is also best in class.
- [01:11:56] Inside the firm: pipeline from events, founder referrals, accelerators and repeat founders, then a screen against mandate, the pitch, diligence alongside a term sheet, and full terms (board seat, reporting rights, who gets paid first). Read those terms carefully.

## Stories and mistakes

- [00:05:20] From her construction years she cites the Lafarge case in Syria, where the company allegedly paid ISIS to protect its assets, as an ethics failure that a public disclosure test would have caught.
- [00:17:05] A whistleblower case in a small team: investigating everyone, not only the known culprit, kept the team in a defensible position.
- [00:22:21] She used to volunteer her own flaws, such as discomfort with public speaking; she now advises leading with confidence and keeping candour for qualified questions.
- [00:46:59] In telecom's golden age, the sharpest finance people saw margins would be cut by new entrants, which is why telecoms set up venture arms.
- [01:09:53] A senior VC partner criticised slow investment decisions online, and founders replied that his own fund had left them waiting six months.
- [01:15:38] A Vodafone panel interview laughed at her "CFO" objective when she was very junior; she now reads writing it down as the start.

## Where it fits the council

- Treasurer: losses are fine only while unit economics improve; never an endless subsidy [00:34:08].
- Capital Sceptic: know exactly how every dollar of the ask will be deployed, and read term sheet rights and who gets paid first [00:33:04].
- Safety Net: dollar money in a local currency business is exposed from day one; hedge, do not convert blindly [00:52:46].
- Conscience: ask how you would feel if the decision became public, and disclose before you are asked [00:08:15].
- Long View: set a stop-loss in advance so a follow-on decision is not made on hope [00:42:24].
- Ambassador: do due diligence on the investor too; the relationship decides the hard years [01:05:48].
