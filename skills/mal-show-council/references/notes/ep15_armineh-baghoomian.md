# Ep 15 · Armineh Baghoomian

Armineh Baghoomian leads PFG's growth lending in the UAE and Saudi Arabia, where her team signed some of the region's first startup credit facilities from 2019. The conversation is a practical guide to debt for founders: how growth debt differs from banks and venture debt, what lenders check before a track record exists, how to read covenants, cost and tenor, and what happens when things go wrong. She also covers Sharia structures, the stresses in global private credit, and why Egypt has been hard for dollar lenders.

## Key takeaways

- [00:00:00] Running a company is the loneliest job there is; the stress spills into how the business grows, and she thinks only people who have done it really understand that.
- [00:03:42] Her team signed its first regional term sheet in 2019, soon after the rules allowing funds to lend were in place, while enforcement was still untested; they took that risk because the rule of law and the framework looked sound.
- [00:05:48] They lend in underserved markets to growth companies that banks will not serve; the alternative for those companies is raising more equity, which costs ownership and control they may not need to give up.
- [00:07:45] Banks want years of audited accounts and run one standard product across many deals, so young, unprofitable firms do not fit; growth lenders split the work into corporate loans for asset-light firms and asset-backed facilities, held in a separate vehicle, for asset-heavy ones.
- [00:10:32] Venture debt usually comes alongside an equity round, follows favoured equity sponsors and asks for warrants. Her fund does growth debt and ignores who the sponsor is; a large minority of its borrowers are founder-owned.
- [00:12:07] Securitisation (selling receivables into an offshore vehicle) is costly, slow and only worth it at large scale; asset-backed finance against a ring-fenced pool with its own bank account is the step before it.
- [00:14:20] A lending platform can never fund its loan book with equity, so it should bring in debt as close to day one as it can; a corporate loan to build the book is riskier because covenants bite before revenue arrives.
- [00:19:15] Before there is a track record, they check the team, the credit and collection policies, the systems to underwrite and monitor loans, and the licences, then ask for a small live deployment to prove the policies are followed, benchmarked against hundreds of deals seen elsewhere.
- [00:21:40] AI-drafted credit policies do not fool experienced lenders. The bigger risk is a gap between written policy and actual practice, and because the facility's eligibility rules mirror the policy, that gap breaks the facility.
- [00:25:50] Most of their borrowers trip a covenant at some point. Seizing the money rarely works, so they negotiate, but they are protecting capital, not running a charity; choose a lender that knows how to work with early-stage companies.
- [00:30:10] The main lesson for equity investors on boards: match the debt's structure to the operating plan. Do not draw a large loan today for acquisitions spread over the next 18 months; you pay for idle money.
- [00:32:50] Bigger bond-style facilities that force a full draw on day one feel safer but cost more; early on, flexibility is often worth paying an extra 100 or 200 basis points for, and setup and legal costs differ widely by structure.
- [00:35:05] Debt was an uncomfortable topic in the region when they arrived. Founders are now more sophisticated but are tempted by the biggest number or the shortest term sheet, and the catches surface later in the documents.
- [00:38:05] To compare offers, ask each lender for every fee and cost, try to cap legal fees (overruns are normal), fix third-party diligence costs where possible, and run the full numbers; equity investors should help with this.
- [00:39:30] Their pricing sits in the same band as other funds and has barely moved in decades, regardless of market rates. Cost falls once a company shows a steadily performing book of meaningful size, at which point equity kickers drop away.
- [00:43:10] Their investors are endowments, pension funds, some sovereign funds and individuals who want steady, lower-risk returns to balance volatile holdings; the fund uses only light back leverage.
- [00:45:05] Typical tenor is 30 to 36 months. Borrowers must be able to repay from the business itself, not from the next equity round, which is why they avoid bridge loans; in practice most borrowers refinance them within two years.
- [00:48:02] Tabby is her model case: equity to test the product, then a first institutional facility scaled fast, refinanced by a larger bank at lower cost, then a stack of lenders for different products. They did not haggle over small terms; they wanted a partner who could scale.
- [00:51:22] With several lenders, the rules on who finances which assets must be crystal clear; ambiguity invites double pledging, which has happened globally.
- [00:53:30] In a workout on a corporate loan they hold a blanket lien (including IP) and work with management to sell, restructure or rebuild; on an asset-backed facility, triggers wind the book down, usually with recourse to the parent. They avoid court where possible and report very low annual losses.
- [00:58:00] When they entered, enforcement had a legal path but no test cases; they lent on confidence in the written rules, and personal guarantees are possible but not standard.
- [01:02:10] Almost all their regional deals are Sharia-structured because founders want it and asset-backed deals suit it; she insists it is done fully, in spirit as well as form, and says returns end up similar either way though the instrument differs.
- [01:05:05] Stress-test by asking what you actually need: why look at a large term sheet when you need a fraction of it, are the milestones achievable, what are the lender's motives, and has the fund actually raised its money.
- [01:08:35] She sees three stresses in global credit: aggressive lending against software recurring revenue, fraud in large syndicated deals where each lender assumed another did the checks, and funds built on retail or insurance money that is forced to deploy. Her fund avoids all three.
- [01:25:40] In Egypt they signed three term sheets and were in documents on one when devaluation hit; they lend in dollars and cannot hedge pound risk cheaply enough, though new structures may reopen the door.

## Stories and mistakes

- [00:25:50] A borrower was in breach for over a year after 18 months of warnings. The product and team were strong; the problem was investors who did not give the right advice in time, and the lender worked with the company to a resolution instead of winding it down.
- [00:32:50] Companies that took bond-style money ring them asking what to do with millions of idle cash they did not need; her answer is to ask their lawyers, because the structure is now their cost.
- [01:13:22] Fraud often starts as a short-term fix under stress ("just for a couple of months") and the hole only grows; that is why her firm built a data agent that checks borrowers' assets live.
- [01:25:40] Egypt's founders were among the strongest they had seen, but a currency devaluation stopped a deal already in documents, because they lend in dollars.
- [01:31:35] She tried law at a firm for two years, hated it, then moved through consulting, operating roles, private equity and private credit; the common thread is building businesses.

## Where it fits the council

- Treasurer: borrow only what the plan needs, drawn when it needs it; idle debt is pure cost.
- Capital Sceptic: compare total cost (fees, legal, warrants, covenants), ask whether the lender has actually raised its fund, and never plan to repay from the next equity round.
- Safety Net: stress-test milestones and covenants before signing; most borrowers trip one, so pick a lender who negotiates.
- Conscience: Sharia structures done fully in spirit, and a gap between written policy and practice is a breach of trust.
- Long View: debt should graduate from corporate loan to asset-backed to a multi-lender stack, as Tabby's did.
- Ambassador: lenders back team, policies and systems first; enforceable security and rule of law decide which markets are open at all.
