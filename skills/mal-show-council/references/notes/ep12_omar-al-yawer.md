# Ep 12 · Omar Al Yawer

Omar Al Yawer began as a teenage high-frequency trader in the dot-com boom, worked in US funds including with John Henry, and came back to the region to build private markets businesses, now at Rüya Partners. The conversation follows his move from three-minute trades to eight-year funds, then explains why banks barely lend to SMEs in the GCC, how Sharia-compliant private credit is structured, and where the market is heading.

## Key takeaways

- [00:01:05] He wants regional banks with local names to offer the kind of financing Western managers are starting to bring in, and hopes the region will use its own capital to keep building at home.
- [00:03:45] At 17 or 18, fresh out of high school in Miami, he joined a new day trading firm that handed young traders half a million dollars at four times leverage. He was among the first five traders; the desk grew to 85 in the dot-com boom.
- [00:06:18] Scalping means taking a slice of an expected move and never waiting for all of it, usually holding for about three minutes. Making tens of thousands by lunchtime at 18 convinced him he had cracked the market, and his family assumed he was gambling.
- [00:09:12] What separated it from gambling was discipline: two hours of charting before and after each session, position sizes that grew in steps from 100 shares, and no more than four stocks at once. Those limits kept a bad day from wiping out the account.
- [00:12:18] He had only ever traded a bull market, so when it turned, the dip-buying rule he had been taught became a trap. The best traders he worked under were steady, with a capped daily gain and a hard ceiling on losses, and avoided the big swings that break most day traders.
- [00:12:18] Getting burnt pushed him towards long-term investing. In private credit, diligence starts with the worst case for the company, not the expected return, which is the opposite of the trading mindset.
- [00:16:28] When the dot-com bubble burst his income fell to near break even within a month. Daily ranges shrank, every enthusiastic trade went nowhere, and costs and slippage ate the account, so he cut size and moved to rules-based algorithmic trading.
- [00:19:38] He expects trading to become almost fully automated. Large firms already run automated risk limits and algorithms that hunt other algorithms, and AI now gives individuals tools that only the big houses used to have.
- [00:22:00] Growing up Iraqi in Miami was easier than people assume because the city's mix let him blend in. Later, US financial advisers who had never met an Arab took him under their wing during the Iraq war.
- [00:25:15] After nine years in the US he came home for a culture where a handshake and a person's name still carry weight. He joined NBK Capital in Kuwait and has worked only in private markets since.
- [00:27:45] He sees the same values across Iraq, Egypt, Kuwait and the UAE, and says talent is now returning to the region for quality of life and safety.
- [00:30:00] SMEs here get funded in one of two ways: a wealthy family behind them, or a bank, which almost never happens. Bank lending to SMEs is tiny compared with the US, so asset-light, cash-generative firms grow slowly on their own money.
- [00:32:28] Banks lend to government-backed projects and to families with land and buildings as collateral. Without hard assets you are mostly shut out, yet demand from growing SMEs has jumped sharply with national visions and the post-COVID move of businesses into the UAE.
- [00:35:35] Banks are stretched by mega projects, and international lenders chase very large energy deals, leaving mid-sized companies to local private credit. Private credit grew after the 2008 crisis when bank rules tightened, and it raises money from sovereign funds, pensions and family offices rather than deposits.
- [00:39:20] Most of Rüya's book is senior direct lending: first in line, four to five year loans shaped around an expansion plan, mixing cash interest, deferred profit and warrants. They avoid lending to lending platforms on risk and regulatory grounds; venture debt goes only to late-stage companies close to profit or listing.
- [00:43:30] For mid-market borrowers they want five years of audited accounts and steady cash flow; for venture debt, Series C or later with institutional backers and little existing debt. They spend about six weeks getting to know the founders, because in a downturn you need a partner who will sit down and restructure honestly.
- [00:46:40] To avoid behaving like a vulture fund, they stick to mature companies and respond to a temporary hiccup by adjusting covenants or granting grace periods. In his view the lender's reputation is the whole business.
- [00:49:15] The fund is fully Sharia-compliant with its own Sharia board, using Murabaha structures backed by real underlying assets. Institutional investors insist on genuine compliance rather than a wrapper.
- [00:52:35] Founders sometimes call private credit expensive, but he compares the all-in return with selling a large equity stake. Debt lets a family keep ownership and board control and repay from future cash flow.
- [00:56:25] Most defaults are operational, such as late receivables or supply chain delays, so they step in early with grace periods or operating advisers. Insolvency reforms in the UAE and Saudi Arabia now allow reorganisation, and a bounced cheque is a civil matter rather than a crime.
- [01:01:35] He says GCC private credit is where private equity stood 15 years ago: tiny against the US and Europe, and set to grow several times over as regional banks co-lend with credit funds and sovereign funds allocate to it.

## Stories and mistakes

- [00:06:18] At 18 he was earning more by lunchtime than his student friends could imagine; his family thought he was gambling, and he dropped dentistry for business and finance.
- [00:12:18] Early wins in a bull market made him believe he had figured the market out, and nobody had taught him how a bear market behaves. He wishes he had then the calm he now gets from breathing, meditation and prayer.
- [00:16:28] When the dot-com bubble burst he went from strong days to barely breaking even in a month, churned his account on trades that went nowhere and, as he jokes, started losing his hair. It humbled him into smaller positions and algorithmic rules.
- [00:22:00] During the Iraq war, US advisers who had never met an Arab took him under their wing, and he wrote newspaper articles in Texas on the collapse of Iraq's economy.
- [00:25:15] Returning to Kuwait and NBK Capital, colleagues welcomed him like family overnight, which settled his decision to stay in the region.

## Where it fits the council

- Treasurer: credit is repaid from future cash flow, so lenders want years of audited accounts and steady cash; most defaults are operational and fixable if raised early (00:43:30, 00:56:25).
- Capital Sceptic: compare the all-in cost of debt with the equity and board control you would otherwise give away; banks only lend against hard collateral (00:52:35, 00:32:28).
- Safety Net: trade inside hard limits, cap the downside, and plan for the worst case before the return (00:09:12, 00:12:18).
- Conscience: a lender that pulls the plug at the first stumble is a vulture; Sharia compliance must be real, not a wrapper (00:46:40, 00:49:15).
- Long View: bull-market confidence is not skill; GCC private credit is early and set to grow sharply (00:12:18, 01:01:35).
- Ambassador: in this region a handshake and a name still count, and lenders pick founders they can trust to restructure openly (00:25:15, 00:43:30).
