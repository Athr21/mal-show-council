# Ep 7 · Wael Nafee

Wael Nafee is an Egyptian born in Abu Dhabi who studied in Canada, worked in Silicon Valley, built a first startup in Egypt, joined Careem early and is now a partner at Raed Ventures. Recorded in Ramadan, the conversation covers why he came back to build in the region, treating fundraising as a last resort, Sharia questions in venture instruments, keeping culture at scale and whether AI is a bubble. It closes on money as an amplifier of character and on constant self-correction.

## Key takeaways

- [00:04:36] He admits part of his reason for a first startup was immature: being a founder looked cool. The right reason is a conviction that the thing has to exist.
- [00:05:56] He left Silicon Valley after five or six years on purpose, before comfort set in, because the region's brightest people go abroad and rarely return. He wants a reverse brain drain.
- [00:07:29] He separates inherited loyalties (country, family) from chosen ones (ideas, faith). His sense of duty to build at home comes from faith he chose again as an adult.
- [00:10:16] He saw startups as a shortcut to impact: big companies took decades, while some startups become massive in five to ten years.
- [00:11:19] Burning cash is right for some models, like social networks that need millions of daily users before ads work, and wrong for models that can earn from day one. The mistake is copying the burn playbook because everyone else does.
- [00:13:06] Self-honesty is a discipline: people asking for advice tend to hear and amplify only the advice they already agreed with.
- [00:13:57] Fundraising should be a last resort, used only when the idea itself cannot happen without it, not as a rescue for a dying business. Many companies can grow on savings, friends and family and early revenue.
- [00:15:11] Interrogate the source of capital, because each source defines success differently. A business paying 15% a year in dividends is a win for most people but close to zero on a VC power-law portfolio; some angels are happy with that return.
- [00:16:47] Banks underwrite on cash flow history and paperwork that young companies lack. Newer SME credit providers use other underwriting methods, but that market is still early.
- [00:19:24] On SAFEs there is no single scholarly view, and alternatives (he mentions an Al-Qard note) exist. Do not give up and default to the conventional route; consult scholars, and sometimes small contract changes fix the issue. If you believe something is impermissible, pause and accept losing the upside.
- [00:20:44] Signs and good feelings come after checking the rulings, not before; the ego can manufacture signs. Feelings have a place, near the end of the decision.
- [00:21:59] For decisions with no right or wrong answer, do the homework, pray istikhara, then trust the outcome. Joining Careem was scary because startups fail often, and he never expected the size of the exit.
- [00:23:47] A super app avoids pushing overconsumption through positioning: "we are there when you need us" rather than "quick, discount now". It is a fine line held by deliberate brand choices.
- [00:25:19] The Careem exit mattered beyond the price: it made many millionaires who became founders and angels, and it proved the region can produce exits, which pulled international investors in for years after. (The answer starts in a line the transcript labels Host.)
- [00:27:39] Careem's mission to simplify and improve people's lives was believed internally, which is why alumni keep backing founders and products that deserve to exist, not as charity.
- [00:29:49] Culture at scale means passing it to new hires fast. Careem's founders used it in daily language ("that doesn't seem very Careem"), ran every joiner through a culture handbook and modelled it in behaviour rather than slogans.
- [00:31:59] A mission must be specific enough to mean something and broad enough for each person to find their own contribution. Careem's handbook took several quarters, with working groups and the leadership team debating each value.
- [00:34:07] He moved to venture because Careem was narrow and deep while capital allocation is broad, and he wanted to multiply the compound effect he saw at Careem, adding operator lessons on what not to do.
- [00:37:53] Outcome over output: judge work by the change it causes, not by whether the planned deliverable shipped. Keep values fixed and means flexible; a fixed mindset is dangerous. (The answer starts in a line the transcript labels Host.)
- [00:40:38] Raed does not chase FOMO. AI is real but full of fluff, as Web3 was, and he asks whether AI is core to the business or decoration in the deck; he expects half or more of AI startups to fail. Like the dot-com crash, the hype will clear and the real models, as e-commerce did, will stay. (Transcript labels the start Host.)
- [00:43:11] He thinks AI is heading into a bubble: some investors add two to three times to a multiple just for AI, founders accept valuations they must then grow into, and down rounds will be the correction. Assumptions from six months ago may already be invalid. (Transcript labels the start Host.)
- [00:45:00] At 16 or 17 he promised himself never to lie to himself; honest self-assessment gets you to the truth faster and saves time. (Transcript labels the start Host.)
- [00:47:07] Friends who asked why he moved back now ask him about jobs in the region. Instability and identity politics in the West are making people reassess assumptions honestly. (Partly in lines the transcript labels Host.)
- [00:53:16] Money is an amplifier: it scales the good in a good person and the worst in someone without values. Keep wealth in the hand, not the heart, and invest in faith before success arrives; the very rich often resist change that threatens the system that made them rich.
- [01:01:37] Inaba means returning even when nothing is wrong, to break autopilot and renew intention. Ramadan works as a deliberate shake-up to rethink, if used for reflection rather than mere survival. (Transcript labels the start Host.)

## Where it fits the council

- Treasurer: build on savings and early revenue where the model allows; burn only as a deliberate, model-specific choice.
- Capital Sceptic: ask what success means to each money source (15% dividends versus power law) and beware AI valuation premiums that force down rounds.
- Safety Net: for a big career jump, do the research first, then decide and accept the outcome; the Careem move shows the risk was real.
- Conscience: check Sharia rulings before feelings, do not abandon values for a "no other choice" instrument, and position products against overconsumption.
- Long View: outcome over output, values fixed and means flexible; the Careem exit's second-order effects on talent and capital.
- Ambassador: spread culture to new hires fast through language, a real handbook and leaders' behaviour; a mission specific enough to mean something.
