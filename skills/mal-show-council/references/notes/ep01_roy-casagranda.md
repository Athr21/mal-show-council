# Ep 1 · Dr. Roy Casagranda

Roy Casagranda is a lecturer who teaches political science and history and ties civilisations, belief systems and economics into one story. The conversation asks why civilisations rise and fall, and covers US economic history, debt, war, the Nordic model, Rome, Egypt, the Islamic golden age and the next generation. His running thread is that societies decline when they grow comfortable, stop adapting and mistake their beliefs for facts.

## Key takeaways

- [00:07:01] Loving your home, people and culture is healthy; nationalism starts when you rank your group above everyone else. He prefers the humbling view that every person and civilisation ends as dust.
- [00:09:53] Civilisations decline like ageing bodies. Two internal causes stand out: wealth breeds laziness ("affluenza"), and people cling to the founding ideas instead of replacing bad ones, which locks them in place.
- [00:10:48] Some shocks sit outside anyone's control. A little ice age in the 1500s and 1600s collapsed food output across Europe, the Middle East and Asia, and some states came through better than others.
- [00:13:29] When a climate shift pushed migrants out of Europe towards Iraq, the hosts chose to feed them rather than fight, and inventing agriculture was how they did it. He reads it as compassion creating a new economy.
- [00:17:26] The US went from a bankrupt start to the largest economy because the 1789 constitution let the federal government tax, but mostly because of geography: vast fertile land and resources, taken brutally, settled with immigrant labour.
- [00:23:00] His reading of 1929: after the First World War, the US kept the European market it had filled, causing a glut; tariffs passed costs to buyers, and firms propped themselves up with a stock market bubble. The crash was the symptom, not the cause.
- [00:25:15] He sees today's AI enthusiasm as bubble-like, with high valuations for loss-making firms, and compares it to the Dutch tulip mania.
- [00:28:12] Citing Piketty, he says there was a single 25-year window, in the post-war US, when ordinary work reliably built wealth; otherwise inheritance or marriage was the route.
- [00:28:17] What ended it: the Vietnam War, back when wars were paid for as they went, and a deliberate policy push from Nixon and later Reagan to shift income back towards the top.
- [00:33:53] US debt matters because interest grows with it and because it depends on foreign, largely oil, money staying confident in the dollar. Printing money is not free: inflation is a hidden tax.
- [00:37:04] Wars are bad for economies. The Second World War was the single exception, because it burned off a Depression-era surplus; a building pays back for generations, a missile pays only its maker.
- [00:41:45] The Nordic model is romanticised but has substance: Sweden came out of 2008 fast and produces far more exported products than its size suggests.
- [00:43:27] Nordic states give a share of state revenue as foreign aid, tied to non-military use, partly to grow future trading partners and partly to reduce pressure to migrate.
- [00:56:49] Rome's long-running model was conquest and plunder (land, slaves, technology), which he calls a Ponzi scheme that only works while expansion continues. Capturing Egypt gave Rome grain, trade and the Great Library's innovation.
- [00:58:06] Rome should have turned itself into a trading power earlier; it adapted too late, and malaria and migrating Germanic tribes finished the western half.
- [01:04:49] In the Ghazali versus Ibn Rushd argument over whether philosophy was worth it, Ghazali won, helped by a run of catastrophes; he links that turn away from open inquiry to later decline, and adds that the Ottoman army later blocked reforms and the empire fell behind.
- [01:06:14] Egypt led much of the developing world in modernising until around 1970, then fell behind: too many people, too few resources, and cities spreading over farmland.
- [01:12:21] The Arab entry into Egypt worked because the Copts, abused by Rome, supplied food, guides and intelligence to a small force; he calls Egypt liberated and Persia conquered.
- [01:21:17] On religious and philosophical violence, his answer is humility: no one can claim to know a supreme being's mind, and science or philosophy cannot contradict it.
- [01:23:19] On Heidegger: name the wrong clearly, then keep what is valuable rather than discarding the whole body of work.
- [01:42:31] Lasting peace can come from leaders. A Roman and a Persian emperor ended centuries of conflict through friendship, and one murder restarted the war. Peace needs a willing partner on the other side too.
- [01:48:04] People mostly see the world through their beliefs, not their senses; travel is what breaks that, by showing your assumptions do not hold.
- [01:52:41] The economic realm has taken over the political (via campaign finance) and the normative (via advertising); his example is how a drinks brand reshaped the image of Christmas.
- [02:11:00] Boomers held power too long and never trained Gen X, so no one was apprenticed to train millennials. Each generation must teach the next.
- [02:12:48] His one practical step for anyone: travel, ideally somewhere far outside your comfort zone, to see that other people are just people.

## Stories and mistakes

- [00:39:15] In the Second World War the US asked citizens to donate stockings, pots and tyres, then threw them away; it was a morale exercise, not a need.
- [00:45:30] A Swedish embassy told him Sweden would not accept asylum applications abroad, but anyone who reached Sweden would be let in.
- [00:49:08] Alaska, bought from Russia, still costs the US more than it earns; he calls it a money pit for now.
- [01:00:22] When Christianity became the only legal religion, mobs burned the Great Library, and Egypt lost its engine for new technology.
- [01:11:16] The 391 attacks in Alexandria were partly a distraction, uniting divided Christian factions against a common enemy.
- [01:12:14] Amr ibn al-As waited to open the caliph's letter until he was inside Egypt, knowing it said turn back unless already there.
- [01:43:46] A Roman general's murder of the emperor Maurice triggered a war that ruined both Rome and Persia after a hard-won peace.
- [02:05:04] Around 200 years ago a farmer found a hidden cave of royal mummies; crowds wept as they were carried away, feeling their own connection to them.
