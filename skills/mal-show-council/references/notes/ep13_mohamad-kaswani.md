# Ep 13 · Mohamad Kaswani

Mohamad Kaswani ran Mortgage Finder inside the Property Finder group for four and a half years and now builds ventures in fintech and proptech. The conversation covers the Syrian tech scene and the Sync initiative, mentorship, how he uses AI with his own data, and a long practical section on renting versus buying in Dubai and how UAE mortgage affordability really works. It closes on how he kept a commission-driven advisory business honest at scale.

## Key takeaways

- [00:00:00] Rent paid once a year is a default risk in its own right: people earn monthly, so asking them to find a full year of rent in one go (tens or hundreds of thousands of dirhams) makes failure more likely than a monthly schedule would.
- [00:02:55] He only discovered Syria's tech scene in early 2025, when a small diaspora trip turned into a 700-person conference in Damascus within about six weeks; that is where Sync (Syria Next Chapter) came from.
- [00:03:54] Sync is a US-based NGO with one mission, 25,000 tech jobs in Syria over five years, and it was quickly named a national initiative and used to connect Syrian officials with large global tech firms.
- [00:06:14] The backers who stood out asked how they could help, not where the deals were; he reads that as the test of genuine support versus investor tourism.
- [00:09:28] Syrian founders built a couple of hundred startups and more than 200 apps with almost no power or internet before December 2024; he treats that kind of resilience as something worth betting on, and believes adversity breeds it.
- [00:14:17] His first mentor, a value investor, taught him to study a business in great depth; he still uses that 20 years on and thinks the region too often judges businesses on optics instead of substance.
- [00:16:10] His mentoring method is simple: listen fully (sometimes for half an hour), then point out what the person has already achieved and ask why the next step should be beyond them.
- [00:18:15] Integrity comes first for him, and he weights relationships over transactions by roughly ten to one. He frames success as luck combined with hard work: with zero work, no amount of luck is enough.
- [00:19:55] He writes his own documents and builds his own data first, then uses AI for structure, research and checking. He dropped one assistant that kept missing its own promised deadlines and moved to Claude, which challenged him and volunteered insights he had not asked for.
- [00:19:55] One such insight: Dubai's worst peak-to-trough fall from 2008 to 2025 was 43%, but excluding the global financial crisis (2015 to 2025) it was under 15%, so "volatile" overstates the risk for a long-term holder.
- [00:23:53] He avoids opinions that are not backed by evidence; "I think" carries no weight, data is what gives the confidence to decide.
- [00:25:12] The crisis-level drop is a once-in-20-to-25-years event and should not drive planning. The deciding factor in buying a home is how long you can hold it; real estate is a long-term asset, not a trade.
- [00:27:30] Dubai is unusual because mortgage rates (under 4%) sit below rental yields (6 to 8%), so owning costs less than renting; stay five years and you can absorb a price fall of 15% or more and still come out ahead.
- [00:27:30] Before anyone buys, he asks whether they can see themselves living there three to five years or longer, because transaction costs of about 7% punish short holds.
- [00:30:30] A home is not just an investment; the stability it buys has real value. A Property Finder survey found a large share of buyers were pushed by a landlord's notice to leave and the jump in rent that follows.
- [00:33:48] Where to live and what to invest in are separate decisions. If you are committed to the city, buy your own home and ignore price swings; he will not sell his at any offer because of his mother's bedroom.
- [00:36:05] Start with a mortgage adviser, before choosing a property: know your limit first, and a pre-approval makes sellers and agents take you seriously.
- [00:37:08] The Central Bank caps total monthly debt payments at 50% of income, and banks test the mortgage as if rates were about 2 points higher, partly because they are still scarred by 2008 and 2009. He finds that frustrating but also reassuring.
- [00:41:04] Credit cards count against affordability on their full limit, not the balance (about 5% of the limit per month), and even a car loan with months left still counts; advisers often tell clients to cut card limits or clear small loans first.
- [00:42:48] Take the longest mortgage term you can: money borrowed at about 4% is cheap next to almost anything else you could do with it. Not all debt is equal, and credit card debt is the dangerous kind.
- [00:44:45] To make advice honest at scale he paid advisers a salary plus a per-deal commission that did not depend on what each bank paid, audited the advice himself, and screened every hire with a test that pits the borrower's interest against the firm's.
- [00:44:45] He reported five-star Google reviews first in every business review and profit last, and says the EBITDA margin still grew about tenfold over his time there.
- [00:50:08] A mortgage with your salary bank can be slightly cheaper but turns painful if you change jobs, because the bank can freeze your account; he does not think the small saving is worth that risk.
- [00:55:38] Stress-test yourself by asking how secure your income is at three levels: you, your company, the economy. Hold six months to a year of cash before a big commitment, while admitting most people, him included, buy their first home stretched.
- [00:58:30] He calls the rule that limits what homeowners may do with released equity absurd: the bank already holds the house as security, and the rule blocks people from clearing expensive debt or investing.

## Stories and mistakes

- [00:14:17] A business-school dean introduced him, then a clueless junior, to a value investor who teased him and then spent real time teaching him; the habit of deep study stayed with him for two decades.
- [00:19:55] An AI assistant kept promising a document "in four hours" and missing it, until hours became weeks; he moved his project to another tool and got unasked-for insights an analyst might have produced.
- [00:44:45] His hiring test for advisers: two bank offers, one better for the borrower and one better for the firm. Anyone who picked the firm's option was out, and the question set the tone from the first meeting.
- [00:52:08] The host (transcript labels this Mohamad Kaswani) collected seven or eight perk credit cards between mortgages, then found they counted against him at their full limits and were far harder to cancel than to get.
- [00:53:15] Paying off a car loan early took about three weeks: told to come in person, then told in the branch after an hour's wait to send an email instead, then left waiting days for the paperwork.
- [00:55:38] He bought his first home under severe financial strain; it worked out, but he is open that his own textbook advice is the ideal, not what most people manage.
- [00:57:25] The host prepaid his mortgage faster than needed, twice, then found new rules stopped him releasing equity freely a third time.

## Where it fits the council

- Treasurer: hold six months to a year of cash before a large fixed commitment, and know which obligations (card limits, short loans) count against you.
- Capital Sceptic: not all debt is equal; cheap long-term debt is worth stretching, compounding card debt at 44% is a trap.
- Safety Net: ask how secure your income is at three levels (you, your company, the economy) before committing.
- Conscience: pay advisers so their advice cannot follow the bank's commission, audit it, and screen hires on that choice.
- Long View: plan around how long you will hold, not around a once-in-20-years crash; Dubai's non-crisis drawdown was under 15%.
- Ambassador: put five-star reviews ahead of profit in every report, and weight relationships over transactions.
