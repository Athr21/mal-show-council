# Ep 8 · Philip Bahoshy

Philip Bahoshy has run Magnet, the region's venture data platform, for about ten years. The conversation reads the first-half funding numbers behind the headlines: thin exits, hidden secondaries, weak listed tech performance, sector and geography concentration, and what the regional conflict is doing to deal flow. He ends with one piece of advice each for policymakers, LPs, VCs, corporates and founders.

## Key takeaways

- [00:04:13] Headline H1 funding was $1.3bn, down on last year. Globally, deal counts have fallen steadily since the 2021 peak while capital concentrates in AI, defence and infrastructure; the region shows the same pattern, with capital flat each quarter thanks to mega and late-stage rounds while Q2 had the fewest deals since 2022.
- [00:06:15] The conflict's effect on venture will show late: a signed deal reflects six to nine months of fundraising, so the real impact lands in Q3 and Q4, though international and early-stage appetite already look softer.
- [00:07:09] Regional venture barely existed as an asset class in 2013 and 2014; it grew because governments in the UAE and later Saudi built fund-of-funds. It is a two-sided market that also needs a steady pipeline of companies moving from seed to Series C, and it is still about 0.2% of GDP against 1% in the US and 2.2% in Singapore.
- [00:10:26] Venture is not charity: it competes with booming stocks, fixed income and real estate, so private money only follows proven exits. The Careem sale created FOMO that spawned new funds, but recent years have lacked meaningful exits.
- [00:11:43] Under 7.5% of funded companies have had an M&A event, some of them fire sales. A quarter of acquisitions came from international buyers and half were larger tech firms buying smaller ones, and both groups may now pull back.
- [00:13:00] Governments must keep doubling down because the region lacks the endowments, pensions and corporate LPs that took 40 to 60 years to form in the US and Europe. Incentives for corporates and family offices as LPs, and VCs telling their story with data, are the levers; meanwhile the US and China AI boom pulls local money abroad.
- [00:17:53] Secondaries are under-reported. Magnet spots them when a round sits far outside the norm (a 50m Series A where about 12m is typical); he guesses up to 30% of announced investment may be secondary, which means primary capital is smaller than it looks. Stigma around founder liquidity keeps it quiet.
- [00:21:09] Alternative capital is rising, often for second-time founders with track records. The old "scale at all costs, be in every MENA country" playbook ended when cheap money did, profitable companies opened doors to credit and venture debt, and strategic corporate money brings its own conflicting incentives.
- [00:24:58] IPOs are not the silver bullet. Magnet's index of 15 listed regional tech companies is flat for the year while the Nasdaq hits records, none sits in global emerging-market tech benchmarks, and regional investors want dividends rather than growth stories. The index gives regulators data instead of VCs simply asking for more listings.
- [00:26:14] Fixes for exits: build global from day one, use government foreign relations to roadshow companies, and get corporates acquiring and investing; corporates supply 12% of regional venture capital against an estimated 18 to 20% abroad.
- [00:35:04] He started the year expecting record M&A and now expects one of the lowest years. After COVID, winners consolidated their sectors in 2021 and 2022, so he expects AI and finance consolidation around 2027 after a slow six months.
- [00:36:04] Look east, not only west: Southeast Asia has similar scale, regulation and exit problems and a longer venture history. Cross-region mergers in areas like Islamic finance, travel and content could make companies listable. Chinese entrants rarely acquire yet; Uber took four to five years before buying, and they have only been here one to three years.
- [00:42:40] VCs without returns back the quickest path to exit, which explains the chain from e-commerce to logistics to fintech. Of roughly 1,000 funds tracked, specialisation is by stage, not sector. Health and education need research money from government, universities and endowments, and LPs with five to ten year patience, before venture can help.
- [00:47:19] Egypt and Jordan lose companies to the GCC because fund-of-fund mandates are geographic, so startups relocate headquarters to reach the money while back offices stay. Egypt's unit economics have been hard and its LP base leans on development institutions; Jordan has engineers but no scale. He floats specialised city hubs instead of every country chasing every sector.
- [00:55:12] Total funding is the wrong success metric. Better ones: the early-stage funnel (to grow 30%, every stage must grow 30%), more exits in the $50m to $1bn range rather than unicorns, employment, revenue as a share of GDP and, above all, fund DPI. International participation differs sharply by market and each story needs reading.
- [01:03:10] Founders expand where others have already shown the way. A Turkish survey on why companies avoid the Middle East had most replies saying nobody had shown them how; Pakistan's ties with Saudi and the UAE grew once a few companies did it.
- [01:07:26] Q2 announcements were largely March deals held back for taste, the same pattern as COVID, and the summer pushes new deals to September. International capital has fallen from half of last year's total to about a quarter, and he expects it to fall further.
- [01:13:15] Venture debt and private equity both need what the region lacks: profitability or clear receivables for debt, and two-to-three-year exits for PE. Most venture debt sits alongside an equity round; regional PE is mostly growth capital rather than buyouts, partly because banks do not lend for leveraged buyouts.
- [01:16:46] Corporate investing struggles because a fund has an eight-year mandate while a corporate manager has one or two year KPIs. Corporates often build in-house to save money, then pay more or abandon it when the sponsor leaves; outsourcing to a specialist VC with a clear mandate is the better model.
- [01:27:18] Family offices hold some answer. In one illustrative geography, hitting a $5bn annual target would need about 15bn of supply against two to three billion of dry powder; capital guarantees or matching schemes, as the UK uses for angels, could draw them in as LPs.
- [01:35:06] AI tools threaten data businesses: chatbots give confident wrong regional numbers with nice charts for $20 a month, and buyers now want speed over depth. Magnet is moving to client-specific dashboards, benchmarking and the story behind the numbers, and weighing MCP or API sales against keeping data in-platform.
- [01:47:02] The typical founder who raises is in their early thirties (about 32), serial founders are easiest to back, and most are non-technical people solving pain points from their careers. Consultants do well because they can tell a story, pitch and follow up.
- [01:50:42] Policymakers should invest during a crisis and find pools of capital; LPs should demand performance data; VCs should give fast yes or no answers.
- [01:52:13] Corporates and suppliers can help founders most by paying on time and being transparent about payments, since cash flow kills more companies than lack of funding. (Partly in the line before.)
- [01:52:59] Founders should publish insights from their own data: content marketing brings leads and AI citations. Magnet's own reports started after PayFort shared clean, simple payments trends from its customer data.

## Stories and mistakes

- [00:40:26] Asked how two burning startups can merge and still raise, he says plainly he has no answer and his own theory may not work. (The admission starts in a line the transcript labels Host.)
- [00:55:42] People have told him his data was wrong because the market felt different; six months later the data matched reality.
- [01:03:10] On a 2019 trip to Turkey, a local data founder tweeted his question and learned that missing role models, not language or politics, was the top barrier.
- [01:17:48] His first consulting job at Oliver Wyman was a bank SME strategy, where most revenue came from a hundred corporates and SMEs were seen as a hassle despite being future corporates.
- [01:38:24] Magnet cut back public research after realising much of it was simply fed into AI machines.
- [01:50:03] He began as a business founder who outsourced the tech and later brought in a CTO while raising.

## Where it fits the council

- Treasurer: corporates paying late can kill a company faster than missing a round; revenue drives the story.
- Capital Sceptic: headline funding hides secondaries, thin exits and flat listed tech; strategic and corporate money carries conflicting incentives and short KPIs.
- Safety Net: in a crisis, international and late-stage money retreats first; plan for deals slipping a quarter or more.
- Conscience: call uncomfortable truths in public, constructively, rather than only telling success stories.
- Long View: the region is in its second venture decade; exits, DPI and specialised hubs matter more than unicorn counts.
- Ambassador: founders who share data-driven content win leads and trust; buyers now value speed and the story behind the numbers.
