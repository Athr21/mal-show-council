# Ep 10 · Jad Antoun

Jad Antoun founded Huspy in Dubai just before the COVID lockdown and has taken it into Spain and on towards Italy, working through real estate and mortgage brokers rather than around them. The conversation covers his appetite for risk, why he chose Europe over the usual regional path, a flat organisation run on P&L owners, hiring, secondaries and buybacks, and why momentum matters more than caution when cash is tight.

## Key takeaways

- [00:03:50] Huspy was incorporated about a week before lockdown and the launch stalled while licences and the land department were closed. They could afford to wait because costs were near zero: two people at a table. Surviving a shock is a balance sheet question of how long you can hold.
- [00:04:50] Leaving a good job to start a company risks less than people think. The worst case is a few months lost and another job; the real danger is spending a year "thinking about it" while the window closes and competition arrives.
- [00:06:00] He only takes the leap when the fundamentals are sound: a strong value proposition for the customer and good margins. A Hail Mary on unknown margins or competition is not something he has the stomach for.
- [00:09:55] Arriving in San Francisco at 18, he thought people in their twenties talking about taking over the world were mad; within months it felt normal. The environment you sit in sets the size of your ambition.
- [00:13:50] He no longer sees $100 million in revenue or a unicorn as big enough. In the Middle East that scale is mostly a market size problem, so a company that wants it usually has to go abroad if the competition allows.
- [00:15:37] Spain was chosen on paper: a nascent sector with no competition, then market structure and size. Madrid was picked as neither too big nor too small for the balance sheet at the time, enough to prove the model in a European city.
- [00:17:30] The big expansion came only once there was a playbook, funded by the Series B with Balderton. Technology and operations repeat from city to city; marketing does not. Going fast then turns into a talent problem.
- [00:19:31] Huspy works for brokers instead of trying to cut them out. In his view none of the 10 to 15 well funded companies that went direct to buyers made it work, because people underestimate how hard brokerage is.
- [00:21:55] AI handles the repetitive work (messages, pulling listings, building PDFs). The offensive use is matching buyers to the right property and lender fast. He tracks agent productivity, what customers save, and how much the network earns.
- [00:25:13] On the ethics of pushing mortgages: reward good behaviour in the marketplace, give brokers the right tools, negotiate exclusive bank rates to pass on, and price from fresh data, since old comparables mislead in a hot market.
- [00:29:25] If a value proposition cannot be said in seconds, either the pitch is badly articulated or the proposition is forced. For agents his line is simply "make more money": top commission, best technology.
- [00:30:25] He runs a flat structure: 15 to 16 direct reports, most of them P&L owners, and he works directly with the top 40 to 50 people. Long tenure in that layer is what lets them launch five cities in 60 days.
- [00:32:41] Flat does not mean fragmented. Operations, marketing and product are central; a feature is built once and switched on or localised per city in a couple of clicks, which keeps operating costs in check.
- [00:35:01] Learning has a time limit. After paying for lessons for a couple of years, saying "we are still learning" every time something breaks becomes an excuse.
- [00:36:26] Acquisitions are judged on one question: are these good people with an asset that more money could grow tenfold? Integration is the one thing done slowly, data and brand included, and deals stay under 5% of top line because organic growth comes first.
- [00:38:13] Recruitment must show the job as it really is. Huspy tells candidates up front the work is intense, so nobody joins on a false pitch and leaves frustrated.
- [00:40:15] The recruitment team hires for open roles and also builds pipelines one or two quarters ahead for cities not yet launched. Scaling fast with the wrong people is how a company falls.
- [00:44:03] First impressions are decided in about five seconds, and a bad one is hard to undo. On UX they count clicks: more than four to complete an action is not elegant.
- [00:46:33] He strongly prefers promoting from inside. It is fairer to staff, lower risk because they know the culture, and it shows everyone a clear path to more pay and equity.
- [00:48:56] Staff treated stock as a cherry on top until the first secondary made it liquid. He now wants secondaries yearly, as an option not an obligation, and has used external buyers as well as buybacks from the balance sheet.
- [00:51:17] Buybacks only made sense once costs were mostly fixed and a new city reached break even in six to nine months. Earlier there were too many variables to plan with confidence.
- [00:53:06] On the last round they raised extra cash simply to sit on the balance sheet. When runway is short he would rather keep momentum than slow down, because momentum is very hard to restart and money follows a good business that is moving.
- [00:54:07] Early rounds run on story; from Series A or B onwards investors judge numbers, cohorts and market size. A weak growth curve cannot be talked around.
- [00:55:22] Risk appetite depends on what is at stake. With 30% of UAE mortgages, he is careful with that unit; a new city is where he takes big risks.
- [00:56:03] He calls himself permanently paranoid and keeps asking why nobody big already owns a market or line before entering it, staying uneasy until growth clearly takes off.

## Stories and mistakes

- [00:03:20] Launching a week before the COVID lockdown: licences took weeks, offices were shut, and the company only survived because it was two people with almost no costs.
- [00:09:55] The culture shock of San Francisco at 18, where outsized ambition went from absurd to normal in a few months and shaped how he builds Huspy.
- [00:15:37] The first year in Spain brought many mistakes, but working through them gave Huspy a launch playbook it can repeat.
- [00:56:03] Huspy went into Spain without understanding labour regulations, learned the importance of marketing on the ground the hard way, and launched a new business unit and two cities at once, doubling the learning load.
- [00:56:49] When AI became hot about two years ago he spoke to around 20 companies a week out of fear that something would disrupt Huspy.
- [00:48:56] Equity grants meant little to staff until the first secondary turned them into real money; that changed how people valued their stock.

## Where it fits the council

- Treasurer: raise extra cash to sit on the balance sheet, and only buy back shares once costs are fixed and city break even is predictable (00:51:17, 00:53:06).
- Capital Sceptic: expand on a proven playbook before raising for it; past Series A the numbers, cohorts and market size decide, not the story (00:17:30, 00:54:07).
- Safety Net: leaving a job costs a few months at worst, but only leap when value proposition and margins are sound (00:04:50, 00:06:00).
- Conscience: equipping brokers to sell mortgages carries a duty to the buyer, met through rewards for good behaviour, better rates and honest pricing data (00:25:13).
- Long View: aim beyond a unicorn, look abroad when the home market caps you, and stay paranoid about disruption (00:13:50, 00:56:03).
- Ambassador: work with the intermediaries customers already trust, hire with an honest pitch, promote from inside and keep marketing local (00:19:31, 00:38:13, 00:46:33).
