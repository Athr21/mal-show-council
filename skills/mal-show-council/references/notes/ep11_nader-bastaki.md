# Ep 11 · Nader Bastaki

Nader Bastaki runs the Dubai Future District Fund, an evergreen fund of funds backed by government money, after a career across corporate and investment banking, government agencies and tourism, and travel to more than 147 countries. The talk moves from what travel teaches about shared values to distribution as the new edge, how a mandate-driven fund chooses managers, and why Dubai's model of accountability, competition and community works.

## Key takeaways

- [00:05:06] The people he connects with most easily are those who have seen the world. Everyone wants the same basics for their family (safety, water, food, education); division mostly comes from narratives built for people, and meeting others in person is what breaks them.
- [00:06:58] He groups people horizontally rather than vertically: national, religious or group identities are easy labels, but underneath, cultures tell the same values in different words. Look for shared incentives first.
- [00:10:21] Whether to build global or local is a question of founder intent, and both are valid. The business itself is rarely the obstacle; reaching the customer is, so bring in people who have already won in that market.
- [00:13:23] Product differences keep shrinking, so distribution is now the main edge. Every product carries its maker's values, and aligning incentives with the partners who will distribute it starts before launch.
- [00:15:11] A shoe that donates a pair for every pair sold wins a customer group on its story, not its build. The narrative can open the distribution channel.
- [00:17:36] Small markets like the UAE and Singapore are fiercely competitive because consumers are spoilt for choice. The scars founders earn there make these places testing grounds for expansion abroad.
- [00:19:29] Wherever government is carrying a burden for citizens (food costs, safety, education), that is the target for entrepreneurs. A private fix frees public money for infrastructure and other enablers.
- [00:22:32] Bringing in VAT and corporate tax made government accountable: to consumers through VAT and to businesses through corporate tax, which is why trade bodies now push hard to take companies global. A fee-based economy also rewards building for markets beyond the border.
- [00:28:47] The fund filters in two stages. First, mandate fit: is the manager or founder doing business here or enabling it from here? Only then come the usual fiduciary tests, including whether the deal fits the portfolio, so not every strong candidate gets in.
- [00:31:12] The fund is evergreen and set aside a dedicated allocation for emerging fund managers as a signal. Newcomers still have to fill a real gap; copying a manager with four successful funds is a hard case to make unless the market is large.
- [00:34:12] He treats investing as a people business more than a pricing one. He looks for founder fire, then the size of the gap, then whether the person's background and team give them the tools to derisk an unproven space.
- [00:36:56] Sector choices follow the government's published Dubai 2033 Economic Agenda: logistics, mobility, property and construction, and finance as an enabler, with health, food tech, agritech and resilience coming through.
- [00:39:30] Headwinds create markets. If supply chains are squeezed, localising them is a business; large companies that cannot pause a working P&L to experiment often end up buying what a founder has tested.
- [00:42:13] The government backed its policy with capital in an evergreen vehicle to signal long-term intent, and chose a fund of funds to back the backers. Information flows up the chain from founder to fund to government, which then adjusts policy.
- [00:45:03] On picking winners: the fund looks first at the health of the whole capital stack, then at mandate fit, and finally at whether the product beats its peers. He frames it as finding a role in the market, not crowding anyone out.
- [00:46:59] The fund's return includes what stays in Dubai: jobs, the induced spending around them, and companies built here. He admits this is hard to measure and their attribution model is still being worked on.
- [00:50:26] People who have visited Dubai see it very differently from those who have only heard of it. He knows many who came to visit and never left; the airport, as the first and last experience, does much of the persuading.
- [00:55:35] Events run for much of the year to tell the story sector by sector, but residents are the strongest advocates. A recommendation from a trusted friend beats any campaign (transcript labels this Host).
- [00:57:59] Common law in the DIFC lowered the barrier to trust for foreign investors, and removing the 51% local ownership rule in 2020 removed another (transcript labels this Host).
- [01:02:47] Intent matters to him because data only looks backwards. In relationships that last decades, shared values are the only forward-looking glue (transcript labels this Host).
- [01:04:22] Rankings beat fixed targets because they move: once you hit a number you stop, but holding a rank means others are always benchmarking against you (transcript labels this Host).
- [01:06:09] Banking taught him to price opportunities; government investing has him building them. Pricing validates work already done, building is the optimist's tool, and each pushes the other (transcript labels this Host).
- [01:12:32] Clustering sectors in free zones, incubators and accelerators is designed to create chance meetings. A community of ambitious people raises the bar rather than blunting effort, because everyone wants to be the standout (transcript labels this Host).
- [01:18:33] His advice to anyone arriving: every place does business differently, so find your tribe. Founders should seek out founders who have done it before (transcript labels this Host).
- [01:29:54] The best way into the fund is a warm introduction from someone both sides trust, as it signals shared values before the first meeting (transcript labels this Host).
